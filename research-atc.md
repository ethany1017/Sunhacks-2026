# Build a runway-crossing safety net, not autonomy

Gus and Ethan should build an advisory, simulation-based "second pair of eyes" for tower coordination, not an autonomous controller. The pain point best supported by the evidence is verbal coordination between controllers, with a surveillance blind spot behind it. That pattern shows up in the 2024 JFK incursion, the March 2026 LaGuardia collision and the June 2026 Boston event, and it is a state-tracking problem a prototype can model. The most defensible demo is a deterministic rule engine that tracks runway and crossing state, plus a push-to-talk voice loop that parses instructions and checks readbacks. An explanation panel shows which rule fired and what state it saw, and a weather event is scripted in. Recommended airports are **KDVT (Deer Valley) as primary and KIWA (Mesa Gateway) as fallback**, with KBUR as a crossing-runway benchmark if the Phoenix-area OSM data is poor. Their draft plan (headless, two-way voice, weather, explanation dashboard, simulation-first) is largely supported. Three parts need narrowing: "autonomous" framing, a Realtime-style voice stack, and live observation, which the data cannot support at the surface. Many figures here come from third-party summaries because FAA and Devpost pages returned 403, so the report flags each one. The Notion page itself was not read; the plan is taken from the task brief's summary.

## The best-documented pain point is controller-to-controller coordination plus a sensor blind spot

Runway incursions are steady rather than spiking. The CRS puts them at about 1,700 a year since 2022, and the DOT OIG counted 1,758 in FY2024, with pilot deviations nearly 62% of them ([CRS IN12675](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IN12675.html); [AVweb](https://avweb.com/?p=384017)). A FAASTeam notice reportedly shows 2025 at just over 1,600 with severe (Category A/B) events up 44%. AVweb's "~13 vs 9" severe count is its own estimate, not an FAA figure, and the FY versus calendar-year basis is unstated. The official FAA FY2025 Category A/B count was not retrieved because the FAA statistics page returned 403. What matters more than the totals is the recent failure pattern. At JFK on April 17, 2024, the NTSB's reported probable cause was nonstandard runway-crossing phraseology ("one at each") and ineffective coordination between two local controllers. Sources disagree on whether ASDE-X alerted: one outlet says it functioned as designed, others say it generated no alert ([AeroCorner](https://aerocorner.com/news/swiss-lx17-jfk-runway-incursion-ntsb-final-report/); [AviationA2Z](https://aviationa2z.com/index.php/2026/10/31/ntsb-reveals-how-three-words-put-five-aircraft-in-danger-at-jfk-airport/)). At LaGuardia on March 22, 2026, ASDE-X did not alert because the fire truck had no transponder and the system could not form a high-confidence track. NTSB found the controller cleared the truck to cross about 12 seconds before touchdown and told it to stop about 11 seconds later ([AIN](https://backend.ainonline.com/aviation-news/aerospace/2026-03-31/asde-x-failed-alert-laguardia-runway-collision); [WJLA](https://wjla.com/news/nation-world/stop-warning-missed-amid-communication-breakdown-in-deadly-laguardia-jet-crash-national-transportation-safety-board-preliminary-report-air-canada-jazz-fire-truck-runway-4-technology-system-failures-air-traffic-control)). That investigation is preliminary, so do not state a cause. Boston (June 20, 2026) rests on a secondary source with investigations open. Austin (Feb 2023) adds a controller with an inaccurate mental model of where the other aircraft was, and NTSB listed the lack of required surface detection equipment as a contributing factor ([NTSB abstract](https://data.ntsb.gov/investigations/Documents/Austin%20Board%20Meeting%20abstract.pdf)).

The people who experience this are tower controllers. The notes' description of positions (clearance delivery, ground, local, flight data) comes from general knowledge of JO 7110.65, not from a retrieved source, so treat it as unverified. What is cited: TFDM is replacing paper strips with electronic flight strips ([FAA TFDM](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/technology/tfdm/efs)). ASDE-X is at 35 airports and ASSC at 8 more, and both are alerting layers that track equipped aircraft and vehicles ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IN12675.html)). Nothing requires ground vehicles to carry ADS-B. Two controllers staffed the LGA cab, and the NTSB preliminary says the local controller was working both ground and tower frequencies ([Pulse summary of NTSB prelim](https://www.pulse.bot/transportation/news/ntsb-prelim-stop-calls-no-alert-before-lga-collision-e44a0f34-fdd1-475c-948b-ec5edca430e2/); an aggregator, NTSB case DCA26MA161). Staffing pressure is real: the National Academies found FAA hired only two-thirds of the controllers its models called for in FY2013-23 ([National Academies](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/actions-from-federal-government-needed-to-alleviate-air-traffic-controller-staffing-shortages-at-many-facilities-says-new-report)). The readback side is also documented: missing hearback usually comes from frequency congestion, and a Eurocontrol study found 52 readback/hearback incidents (about 10% of its sample), mostly undetected ([SKYbrary](https://skybrary.aero/index.php/Read-back_or_Hear-back)).

Delay is the weaker story. ATD-2 gate-hold metering at Charlotte saved about 3.1M lb of fuel and 414.4 hours of surface delay over its first two years ([NASA](https://nasa.gov/aeronautics/nasa-showcases-benefits-of-air-traffic-management-tools)). But an ASPM comparison found no significant pre/post change in total taxi-out time ([NASA ATD-2 comparison](https://aviationsystems.arc.nasa.gov/publications/atd2/tech-transfers/4_Evaluation_Results/4.03%20ATD2_TaxiTimeComparison_v1_20180410.pdf)). Metering moves waiting from the taxiway to the gate rather than eliminating it. Third-party BTS analysis attributes only about 22% of delay minutes to the NAS, and most of that is beyond an airport-level agent ([AirAdvisor](https://airadvisor.com/en/statistics/north-american-airlines-late-arrivals); unverified against BTS). So any throughput claim should be modest, and the notes' own ranking puts the runway-crossing and readback monitors first (judgment, not sourced), with gate-hold metering second.

## Existing systems keep a human in command and use AI only as an advisory layer

Digital towers are deployed but are not autonomous. London City went fully remote in April 2021 ([NATS](https://www.nats.aero/our-solutions/digital-towers/)). Sweden's Ornskoldsvik tower was approved in 2014 ([Saab](https://www.saab.com/newsroom/press-releases/2014/saab-remote-tower-technology-receives-final-operational-approval-from-swedish-transport-agency)). Doha's hybrid virtual tower uses Searidge's Aimee AI engine for detection and safety alerts ([ATC Network](https://www.atc-network.com/atc-news/searidge-technologies/hamad-international-airports-virtual-tower-goes-into-operation)). Vigo became Spain's first regulator-certified digital tower in December 2025 ([Airport Improvement](https://airportimprovement.com/article/searidge-powers-spains-first-regulator-certified-digital-control-tower-at-vigo-airport/)). The Doha date is slightly uncertain: Searidge's summary says Feb 2025, but the AIN article is dated Dec 2025. Searidge's "50+ sites in 25 countries" is a vendor claim. The US lagged: the FAA's Leesburg pilot ended in June 2023 ([AIN](https://ainonline.com/aviation-news/business-aviation/2023-06-26/leesburg-moving-its-remote-tower)) and Fort Collins-Loveland work was paused in October 2023 ([GA News](https://generalaviationnews.com/2023/10/26/works-paused-on-remote-control-tower-at-kfnl/)). In 2026 the FAA made a direct award to Frequentis USA for digital remote towers ([HigherGov](https://www.highergov.com/contract-opportunity/the-federal-aviation-693ka8-26-d-00018-award-693ka8-26-d-00018-frequentis-usa-inc-39859/)). The notes found no authoritative post-mortem on why the US pilots stalled, and the FAA's stated reasons are a gap.

On AI, the deployed pieces are vision and alerting (Aimee, trialed at Heathrow as explicitly non-operational with the controller deciding) and strategic flow tools. FAA's June 2026 award to Air Space Intelligence is reported at USD 875M over 12 years, for flow management rather than tower clearances ([Nextgov](https://www.nextgov.com/acquisition/2026/06/faa-awards-software-and-ai-contract-part-air-traffic-control-modernization/414354/)). Voice AI is research or early-stage: SESAR's HAAWAII targeted readback error detection, and earlier ASR trials struggled with accents and off-phraseology speech ([CORDIS](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/884287)). Startups NoamAI and Enhanced Radar (USD 7M round, May 2026) are company claims with no regulatory approval found ([AIN](https://backend.ainonline.com/aviation-news/aerospace/2025-05-16/noamai-launches-atc-assistant-airspace-world); [Pulse](https://www.pulse.bot/ai/deals/initialized-leads-7m-funding-round-for-enhanced-radar-52f12017-b349-41d3-9d45-cef46a546670/)). The notes found no regulator-approved AI that issues or authorizes clearances. Not covered: Raytheon, Thales, Airbus internal work, DARPA/AFRL, MITRE and Eurocontrol AI programs. The details and measured error rates of a 2026 arXiv paper on LLM-based ATC were not read.

The regulatory frame is safety-risk management, not an "AI certification." The notes correct the task brief on this point: FAA Order 1100.161B governs independent AOV oversight of the ATO and is not an automation-certification standard ([FAA](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Order/FAA_Order_1100.161B.pdf)). Safety risk management sits under Order 8040.4C ([FAA](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Order/FAA_Order_8040.4C.pdf)). EASA's AI Concept Paper Issue 2 covers Level 1 (assistance) and Level 2 (human-AI teaming), with explainability as an explicit expectation ([EASA](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/newsroom-and-events/news/easa-publishes-artificial-intelligence-concept-paper-issue-02-guidance)). An ICAO paper describes Level 3 and attributes a roughly 2035 EASA target to it, but the text is internally inconsistent and EASA's own page does not confirm it, so the date is unverified ([ICAO APAC](https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/APAC/Meetings/DGCA/59th%20DGCA%20Meeting-%20Cebu%20Philipines-%2014%20to%2018%20October%202024/03-%20Information%20Papers/IP-03-05-EASA-EASA-S-APPROACH-TO-ARTIFICIAL-INTELLIGENCE-IN-AVIATION.pdf)). The statement that DO-178C does not apply to ground ATC (ED-109A/ED-153 instead) is background knowledge and unverified. No source on legal liability for AI-assisted ATC errors was found. The architecture that follows from this is the notes' inference, not a stated rule: ASR and LLM do perception and drafting only, a deterministic engine validates, and a human confirms.

## What the plan gets right, and where the research narrows or contradicts it

| Draft-plan element | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Headless architecture | Supported | A custom headless discrete-event sim fits a 24-hour build better than adopting a framework. |
| Two-way voice | Supported, narrowed | Push-to-talk batch pipeline, not the Realtime API. |
| Weather integration | Supported, narrowed | AWC works but has no CORS and a 30-day window; the haboob should be a scripted injection. |
| Explanation dashboard | Supported, strengthened | It matches EASA's explainability expectation and is the main differentiator versus Devpost voice-ATC projects. |
| Simulation-first, live later | Supported, with a hard limit | Live surface data cannot be obtained, so "live mode" means approach/departure demand only. |
| "Autonomous" framing | Contradicted | No regulator pathway; every deployed system keeps a human. |

On the simulator, the notes recommend building a lightweight Python event-driven sim (SimPy or a heap-based loop) on an OSM-derived taxiway graph with a fixed seed and append-only JSONL event log. BlueSky is MIT and actively maintained (last push 2026-10-12) and can run detached from Python, but it has no maintained core taxi support ([GitHub](https://api.github.com/repos/TUDelft-CNS-ATM/bluesky)). OpenTaxi (MIT, taxiway graph, conflict detection, Gymnasium env) is the closest purpose-built tool, but it has 2 stars and a two-month history, so treat it as a reference to vet, not a dependency ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/opentaxi/)). openScope has no documented API or ground ops. GPL licensing (ASSET2, FlightGear, ATC-pie) matters if they want a permissive release. Unverified: whether BlueSky's taxi plugin ships or runs, and OpenTaxi's runway-occupancy handling.

On voice, off-the-shelf Whisper is poor on real ATC radio (untuned Whisper-small was 63.32% WER on ATCO2), and fine-tuning brings it to roughly 13-23% on that noisy corpus and to low single digits on clean scripted speech ([arXiv 2510.24570](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.24570v2); [TU Delft](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:8e02d222-5775-441d-94d2-96c26156cf43)). WERs are not comparable across datasets. The community checkpoint `jacktol/whisper-large-v3-finetuned-for-ATC` claims 6.5% WER, but that is self-reported on the author's own test set ([HF](https://huggingface.co/jacktol/whisper-large-v3-finetuned-for-ATC)). Hackathon audio will be TTS-generated and clean, so any strong ASR should do well, and the real risks are numbers, callsigns and phonetic letters. The notes found no ATC WER for Deepgram, AssemblyAI, Google, Azure, AWS or NVIDIA models, so a 20-utterance bench on their own audio is needed. The recommended pipeline is browser push-to-talk, `gpt-4o-mini-transcribe` (listed at $0.003/min by a third-party price page, [costgoat](https://costgoat.com/pricing/openai-transcription)) with a prompt listing active callsigns and runways, a normalizer for niner/tree/fife, a grammar parser with fuzzy callsign matching, templated pilot readbacks, TTS and a bandpass radio filter. The batch latency budget of about 1-3 seconds is the notes' estimate, not a measurement, and the "niner/fife" entries in JO 7110.65 could not be confirmed. A stretch goal is injecting wrong-digit, wrong-runway, omitted hold-short and wrong-callsign errors into pilot readbacks so verification has ground truth. The same TU Delft thesis reports command extraction F1 of 0.97 for callsign but only 0.82 for command and 0.87 for value (excerpts only), which is why the closed-world parser matters. TTS and ASR prices come from third-party aggregators, and OpenAI's gpt-4o-mini-tts shutdown date (reported January 6, 2027) is unconfirmed.

On data, the free, no-approval set is AWC weather, OSM, FAA NASR/CIFP/d-TPP downloads, BTS, adsb.lol, airplanes.live (non-commercial) and OpenSky live with a free account. OSM is the practical source for a taxiway graph because the notes found no national FAA taxiway dataset and the FAA runway layer appears to be endpoint-based (unconfirmed) ([OSM wiki](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Tag:aeroway%3Dtaxiway); [catalog.data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/runways)). OSM completeness at specific airports was not assessed, and OSM `gate` and `parking_position` are separate concepts, so stand refs will be uneven. AWC needs no key, caps at 100 requests/min and 30 days of history, and has no CORS, so it needs a backend ([AWC](https://aviationweather.gov/data/api/)). NWS, Open-Meteo and CheckWX were not researched. OpenSky anonymous users get 400 credits a day and standard accounts 4,000, and history via Trino is free only for academic or government users after an approval of undocumented length ([OpenSky REST](https://openskynetwork.github.io/opensky-api/rest.html); [Trino](https://openskynetwork.github.io/opensky-api/trino.html)). SWIM needs signed agreements. ASDE-X data has no found public access. Ground ADS-B coverage is patchy, since surface surveillance is normally purpose-built MLAT and structures degrade reception ([MITRE/FAA WAM](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/about/office_org/headquarters_offices/ato/WAM_WhitePaperFINAL_MITRE_v2.pdf)). The implication is that surface traffic must be synthetic, with real data used only for demand shape and approach/departure flows. The "live observation later" mode is therefore an arrival/departure demo, not a ramp view, and the doc should say so.

## KDVT primary, KIWA fallback, and a four-minute demo that proves checks rather than autonomy

KDVT has a long main runway (7R/25L, 8,196 ft) and a short training runway (7L/25R, 4,500 ft), a tower, and very high general-aviation traffic ([AirNav KDVT](https://airnav.com/airport/KDVT)). Traffic figures conflict. One source cites 490,702 operations for 2025 while the airport's partial 2025 sheet shows 257,280 year-to-date, and none is ATADS-verified ([DVT 2025 stats](https://www.deervalleyairport.com/media/nyvpt4qt/annual-2025-stats-dvt.pdf)). The layout is small enough to model and busy enough that 8-15 aircraft is realistic. Whether KDVT has an FAA hot-spot entry is only suggested by a published hot-spot PDF whose contents were not retrieved. KIWA has three parallels and little crossing complexity, which makes it the safe second config ([AirNav KIWA](https://airnav.com/airport/KIWA)). KPHX is the recognizable name, but with three runways, one crossing and a long taxiway system it is the hardest to model correctly in 24 hours. The documented KPHX hot spots (HS 1 and HS 2 around Taxiway F and 07L/25R crossings, from a June 2025 listing whose current-edition status is unconfirmed) are useful as a grounding slide, not as the live scenario ([FAA hot spot list](https://www.faa.gov/aero_docs/afd/12jun2025/sw_hotspot.pdf)). KBUR has genuinely intersecting runways and is the benchmark if the Phoenix fields have bad OSM data. KSAN was not researched.

The research narrows the proposed scenario. The notes' draft is a four-minute simulated monsoon haboob at KDVT with 10-12 aircraft, a deterministic planner, an LLM used only for parsing and phrasing, and every instruction rule-checked and logged. That scope is sound, but it should lead with the documented pain point. Open with a hold-short and runway-crossing sequence where a wrong or missing readback is caught and an unsafe crossing clearance is refused, then inject the haboob. The Aug 25, 2025 Sky Harbor event is the precedent: a ground stop of roughly 30-60 minutes depending on the report, with diversion and delay counts that vary by outlet ([View from the Wing](https://viewfromthewing.com/dust-storm-tore-through-phoenix-sky-harbor-grounding-all-flights-ripping-open-parts-of-terminal-roof); [KVIA](https://kvia.com/news/2025/08/25/massive-dust-storm-slams-phoenix-causing-damage-and-delays-at-the-airport-and-knocking-out-power/)). Closing the training runway and re-sequencing under reduced visibility is a plausible replan beat, but no source supports a density-altitude effect at KDVT, so keep that out as a fact. The explanation panel (rule fired, state snapshot, rejected alternatives) is the differentiator. The Devpost field is crowded with voice-plus-LLM projects (MayDay, ATC Agent, Navda, ATC Trainer, ATCmonitor and others), but those pages returned 403, so awards and what judges rewarded are unverified ([MayDay](https://devpost.com/software/mayday-l74dub)). The proposed acceptance metrics (zero separation losses over 20 seeded runs, 100% of instructions traceable to a rule and state, readback errors caught in 9 of 10 scripted tests, replan under 2 seconds, 90% intent parse on 30 utterances, honest delay versus FCFS) are targets, not tested numbers. Wake-separation values must be copied from FAA JO 7110.126; the RECAT matrix could not be extracted and no runway-occupancy or taxi-speed figures were researched.

Framing is part of the product. Say "simulation-based decision support prototype, not certified, not for operational use, human confirms every instruction, LLM never issues clearances." Avoid "autonomous air traffic control," "replaces controllers," or any unqualified accuracy number. The notes' inference is that the framing mirrors industry practice and EASA's Level 1/2 vocabulary. Show failure handling on stage: low-confidence ASR flagged for a human, a data-staleness indicator (the LGA lesson is that the tool must show what it does not see, such as a non-transponder vehicle), and a "tool off, operations continue" fallback.

The event itself is the largest schedule unknown. The last confirmed SunHacks (Sept 27-28, 2025) gave about 23 hours of official build time for teams of 1-4, and SunHacks calls itself a 24-36 hour event ([State Press](https://www.statepress.com/article/2025/10/scitech-sun-hacks-hackathon)). No 2026 date, tracks, rubric or eligibility was found, and a September-style 2026 edition may already have passed since today is November 14, 2026. The eligibility note (ASU students or recent alumni) should be confirmed for both teammates. Plan for about 23 hours, not 48, and treat the 48-hour additions (KIWA second config, hot-spot warning layer) as optional.

## Conclusion

The research pushes the project away from "autonomous airport control" and toward something more credible and more demoable: a verifier. Every real system in this space, from Doha's virtual tower to Heathrow's trial, keeps a controller in charge and uses AI to see or flag. The recent accidents are failures of shared state and phraseology, which a deterministic checker with visible reasoning addresses directly. The differentiator versus other hackathon voice-ATC entries is therefore evidence: replay, a rule trace, a refused unsafe clearance, and honest metrics including cases where the planner loses to FCFS.

Two constraints should shape scope on day one. Surface truth cannot be had from public data, so synthetic surface traffic is the only option, and "live mode" can only mean demand and approach flows. And a large share of the supporting numbers in this report are third-party or preliminary, so none should appear on a slide as fact without the verification below.
